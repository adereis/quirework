Read `variant-common` first: it holds what every edition keeps, and how

The six-pager retells the book as one narrative memo for Hedda Count,
the kind her company reads in silence at the start of a meeting. It is
one document, so it is drafted whole: the variant policy gives the kind
`shape = "document"`, and its one draft lands in
`variants/six-pager/_raw/document.md`.

Compose it as `variants/six-pager/memo.qmd`, with its sidecar at
`variants/six-pager/_marks/memo.md`. Six pages is a ceiling, in the
rendered PDF; a memo that fits in fewer is better. Check the page count
after `publish build`, not the word count before it.

A memo is a stranger form for this book than a chapter, so three things
get checked twice. It is addressed to Hedda, so it never tells her what
she thought or felt: only what she did and said, as the book has it. It
asks her for nothing and proposes nothing, since the book does neither.
And it keeps the order of Cy's three answers, and the fourth, because
that order is the book's spine.

## Blind-draft brief

The reader is Hedda Count: the manager in this book, who has asked
"How's the book?" at the end of every one-on-one. This edition is the
long answer, written by the narrator, for her, as a narrative memo of
the kind her company reads in silence at the start of a meeting: she
will read it at the table, once, start to finish, before anyone speaks.

Write it in the narrator's first person, to Hedda, as "you". Where the
story is about her, say only what she did and said, as the book tells
it; never what she thought, felt or noticed.

Full paragraphs, complete sentences, prose that argues and tells in
order. No bullets, no numbered lists, no tables, no bold lead-ins, no
summary box. Use a few `## ` headings, four to six, each a plain phrase
for what happens in its part, the way a chapter is titled; never a label
such as Background, Problem, Solution or Next Steps, and never anything
that would look at home on a slide.

It is at most six printed pages, about three thousand words, and shorter
is better. Compress scenes; do not drop the book's people, its numbers
or its doubts to make room. Propose nothing and ask her for nothing: the
memo answers her question, at length, once.
