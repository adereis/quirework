# What this book holds

The commitments a second writer would break without noticing. The book
is a short comic memoir: a preface and nine chapters in three parts.
Everyone's name is changed, and everything that deserved it is
exaggerated.

## The side quest is the subject {#side-quest}

The book is about not finishing a book. Cy spent three years on Full
Stop, a natural history of punctuation: where each mark came from, and
who spread it. It stopped eleven chapters in. Instead of finishing it,
Cy built Quirework. That detour, not Full Stop, is what this book tells.

## Claims that lived only in sentences {#claims-in-sentences}

Full Stop did not fail at the sentences. It failed because its claims
lived only in sentences, scattered across eleven chapters, with nothing
holding them together. Two Shops ends where the contradiction stops
being about two shops, without saying so; Index Cards is the first
chapter to say it out loud.

## Two shops, one habit {#two-shops}

Chapter four of Full Stop, on the dash, says one printer's shop made the
dash fashionable. Chapter nine, on the semicolon, credits a rival shop
with the same habit. Fixing four breaks nine, and fixing nine breaks
four. Each fix needs a footnote in the other chapter, until the
footnotes cite footnotes. For one week Cy decides both shops did it,
independently, at the same time, and it is the happiest week of the
book; it ends because chapter four's title, "A Fashionable
Interruption", has the word "fashionable" in it, and fashion does not
happen twice. The disagreement does not stay between two chapters:
chapter two credits "the printers of the period", and chapter seven
agrees with the wrong shop without being asked. The shops are never
named. The number of rounds is never given: Cy did it more times than
they will admit in print.

## The notes escalate {#notes-escalate}

Cy keeps notes, and each kind outgrows the last. First index cards, one
claim per card, in a shoebox; one falls behind the radiator and turns up
in March. Then a spreadsheet of three hundred and twelve rows,
color-coded until the colors need a legend. Then a forty-line script
that checks whether each note still matches the sentence it is about.
Its first run finds fourteen contradictions, not one. The script grows,
becomes a tool, and gets a name. Full Stop does not get an ending.

## An assistant is worth what you can check {#worth-what-you-check}

An assistant is useful exactly as far as you can check it, and the
compositor is the proof. Otto Complete once supplied a quotation from a
seventeenth-century compositor: "A Dash is but a Comma that hath lost
its Nerve." Cy cited it for two weeks and read it aloud to people who
had not asked. The compositor never existed. The quotation is given
whole, spelled and capitalized exactly as Otto gave it.

## The name came from Otto {#the-name}

Around two in the morning Cy typed "quirk" into a chat, Otto finished it
as "quirework", and Cy liked it better than anything they would have
chosen.

## No villains {#no-villains}

Nobody in this book is a villain, Otto and Em included. Otto is a
mirror: eager, agreeable, quick to finish Cy's sentences, and the
compositor happened because Cy wanted that sentence very much and Otto
could tell. The fault the book finds is in where the claims lived, not
in any person or program.

## Em is never asked {#em-never-asked}

Every message from Em Dash contains three em dashes, never two, never
four. Em once replied to a meeting invite with a haiku. Cy suspects Em
is a language model and has never asked, and the book never settles it.
The question stays unasked to the end, and that is how Em's part of the
story closes, not a gap in it.

## Em's dashes stay off the page {#em-dashes-off-the-page}

The prose contains no em dash anywhere, so Em's are counted and never
shown. A message from Em is reported or paraphrased, never quoted with
its punctuation intact.

## The people who ask about the book {#people-who-ask}

Hedda Count, Cy's manager, is kind and busy and ends every one-on-one
with "how's the book?" Cy's answers go from "chapter four" to "I'm
building something for chapter four" to a live demo, in Hedda's office,
on Hedda's monitor, of the tool catching Cy's own book disagreeing with
itself. Hedda watches all of it and asks whether the book is done. Hedda
keeps a running document for their one-on-ones, copied forward each
time, and its line "Book: ch. 4" survives every copy.

Red Penn, Cy's editor at a small press, waits three years for chapter
twelve and gets a nine-hundred-line README. Red reads all of it and
writes back one line: "Is there a chapter in here?" Until then Red's
emails are short: the subject line "Checking in", and a body that says
"Checking in" and nothing else.

Neither is a foil. Each asks the one question the book cannot answer
yet, and each is right to.

## The tool is not the book it was built for {#not-the-book-it-was-built-for}

A tool built to avoid a book can still be how a book gets finished; it
just is not the book it was built for. The book Quirework finishes is
this one. Full Stop stays unfinished. The memoir's last chapter is
called Full Stop because that is where the memoir stops and that book
does not. Nothing before that last chapter says how the book ends.

## Written with the tool it is about {#written-with-the-tool}

This book is written with Quirework, and the preface admits it. The
irony is known to the author and used on purpose: the book counts on it
rather than apologizing for it. The preface also says, once, in one flat
sentence, that every name is changed and everything that deserved it is
exaggerated: that is where the book admits it is unreliable. It does not
claim to be fully consistent either; the tool caught most of its
disagreements, not all.
