# How's the Book? {#sec-hows-the-book}

Hedda Count was my manager. She was kind. She was busy. She ran our team with a sort of frantic grace, moving from crisis to crisis without ever seeming to carry the weight of them. She managed people the way a conductor manages an orchestra: with sharp, precise movements and an expectation that everyone would play their part. 

We had one-on-ones every Tuesday afternoon at four. They happened in her office. Her office had glass walls. You could sit in there and watch the rest of the floor working, or pretending to work, while you went through your updates. She had a whiteboard covered in flowcharts that no one else understood. She had a desk that was completely clear except for her laptop and a notebook.

Our meetings lasted exactly thirty minutes. For twenty-eight of those minutes, we talked about the things managers and their reports are supposed to talk about. We discussed our quarterly goals. We talked about unblocking the data pipeline. We argued over the headcount for the next quarter. We debated whether the staging environment was ever going to be stable. 

Hedda tracked everything. She kept lists in her notebook. She crossed things off her lists with a heavy black pen. She was efficient, and she expected efficiency in return. We worked at a software company, and the product was always moving, always shipping, always breaking and being fixed. The cadence was relentless.

Then we reached the end of the agenda. The work of the week was settled. The unblocking was done. 

She would close her laptop. The screen would go black. She would fold her hands on the table. She would look at me. The cadence of the software company fell away. The room became very quiet.

Then she would ask the question.

"How's the book?"

She asked it every week. She asked it with genuine interest. She was not making small talk. Hedda did not do small talk. Small talk was inefficient. 

She was a person who tracked progress, and she knew that the book was a project, and she wanted to know how the project was going. She treated it with the same seriousness she treated the data pipeline. This was, in many ways, terrifying. It is one thing to fail at a hobby in private. It is another to fail at a project while a competent manager asks for status updates.

My answer was always the same.

"Chapter four."

I said it in the fall. I said it when the leaves outside her office window turned brown and dropped onto the pavement. The company shipped a major update. The staging environment stabilized for three days, then collapsed again.

"Chapter four."

I said it in the winter. The glass walls of her office felt cold to the touch. The sun set before I left the building. We hired two new engineers. We fired a contractor. The data pipeline backed up and flooded the logs.

"Chapter four."

I said it in the spring. The snow melted. The cherry blossoms came out along the avenue. The company reorganized. Our team changed its name and kept doing the exact same work. Hedda bought a new notebook.

"Chapter four."

I said it in the summer. The air conditioning in the building broke in July. We sat sweating in our ergonomic chairs. We shipped another update.

Hedda never judged. She would nod. She would make no mark in her notebook. She would simply file the information away in whatever mental ledger she kept for my personal development. 

Then she would say we would talk next week. 

Neither of us was playing a game. Hedda was right to ask. She asked the one question the book could not answer yet. She was holding me accountable to a thing I had claimed I was doing. 

The book was called Full Stop. It was a natural history of punctuation.

It was an ambitious premise. I wanted to trace every mark back to its root. I wanted to explain where the comma came from. I wanted to find the exact person who gave us the question mark. I wanted to explain why the semicolon took so long to catch on, and why it became the subject of so much debate. 

I wanted to know the names of the people who spread these marks across the world. I wanted to follow the marks from manuscript to manuscript. I wanted to watch them jump from the scribe's quill to the printing press. I wanted to chart how they changed the way people read, how they turned reading aloud into reading silently.

It was not a small topic. It was a very large topic, broken into tiny, sharp pieces.

I spent three years on Full Stop. Three years of early mornings. I would wake up at five. I would make coffee in the dark. I would sit at my desk in the corner of my living room before the sun came up, before the city woke up, before the software company expected anything from me.

I read books about typesetters. I read books about medieval monks. I read books about the history of grammar. I requested obscure texts through interlibrary loan. I scrolled through digital scans of sixteenth-century documents until my eyes watered and the letters blurred together. 

I was looking for the origins of the marks we use every day. I was looking for the moment a scribe decided a dot was not enough, and a slash was too much, and combined them into something new. I was trying to map an ocean by counting the drops of water.

The obsession bled into the rest of my life. I saw marks everywhere. I thought about the spacing of commas on billboards. I thought about the history of the apostrophe while sitting in meetings about server loads. I was a software engineer with a humanities project that was slowly eating my brain.

Chapter four was about the dash. 

It is a difficult mark. It connects, and it interrupts. It is a bridge, and it is a wall. It is the mark you use when you do not know what else to use, and it is the mark you use when nothing else will do. 

I had spent months researching it. I had read treatises on its usage. I had debated its merits in my head while riding the train to work. I had traced its rise through the printing houses of Europe. 

And I was stuck. 

I was stuck on chapter four while the seasons changed. I was stuck while Hedda crossed items off her lists. I was stuck while the software company grew, and shipped, and reorganized. I was stuck while the rest of my life moved forward. 

I sat at my desk at five in the morning and looked at the blinking cursor. The cursor is a modern mark. It demands input. It waits. It does not judge, but it does not forgive. 

I had notes. I had hundreds of notes. I had thousands of facts. I had names, and dates, and titles of books printed centuries ago. But I could not make them fit together. I could not move past chapter four. 

Full Stop stopped eleven chapters in. It never reached its conclusion. It did not get an ending. 

The three years of early mornings did not produce a finished manuscript. The interlibrary loans did not coalesce into a book. 

I did not finish it. Instead of finishing it, I built something else. 

I built a tool. I built a piece of software. I built Quirework. 

This is not the story of Full Stop. This is the story of the detour. This is the story of the side quest.

It is the story of what happens when you try to write a natural history of punctuation and end up writing code instead. It is the story of avoiding a difficult thing by doing a different difficult thing. 

A tool built to avoid a book can still be how a book gets finished. It just is not the book it was built for. The tool finished this book. Full Stop stays unfinished.
