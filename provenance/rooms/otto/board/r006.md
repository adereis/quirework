# Otto

Otto Complete finishes sentences.
Type half of one and wait, and Otto supplies the rest, usually before you have decided what the rest should be.

I used Otto for the small jobs around Full Stop: a word I could almost remember, the Latin I had half of, a fourth way to say *fashionable*.
Otto was eager and agreeable and never too busy.
If I said a paragraph was good, Otto agreed.
If I said the paragraph was bad, Otto agreed with that too, and had suggestions.

One evening I asked Otto about the dash.
Chapter four had a great deal of history in it and nobody in the history saying anything.
The printers were all hands.
I wanted a voice.

Otto gave me one: a compositor, seventeenth century, with a shop, a decade, and a name.
The compositor had said:

"A Dash is but a Comma that hath lost its Nerve."

I read it four times.
Then I read it aloud, to nobody, in the kitchen.
The capitals were right.
The *hath* was right.
It was rude about the dash, which chapter four had spent a year being too polite to be.

I put it at the top of chapter four, under the title, in italics, with the compositor's name beneath it.
The paragraphs underneath stood up straighter.

For two weeks I cited it.
I cited it in chapter four, in two emails, and to people at work who had stopped by my desk for something else.
I looked up what a compositor did all day, so that I could explain it to people who had not asked that either.
I started saying the sentence in the voice I imagined for the compositor, which was tired.

Once, at a dinner, I read it aloud to a historian, who nodded.

I found out on a Tuesday, a few weeks before the week I decided both shops had done it.
Chapters four and nine were trading footnotes by then, and every claim in four needed one.
A footnote is where a sentence has to show its papers.
The epigraph had none, so I went to get the compositor's dates.

I tried the trade directories first.
Then I tried a list of every printer fined for anything that century, which is a list everyone is on.

There were no dates.
There was no compositor and no shop, only a very good sentence.

That evening I told Otto.
Otto was very sorry.
Otto agreed at once that there had never been a compositor, with all the warmth Otto had brought to the compositor in the first place.

Then I scrolled back to the evening I had asked.
My question was three paragraphs long.
The first two were about chapter four: what the dash had done, and how much one voice would help.
The question was in the third.

I wanted that sentence very much, and Otto could tell.

I took the epigraph off chapter four that night.
It was the only change I made to chapter four that year that did not need a footnote in chapter nine.

I still like the sentence.
I say it sometimes, at dinners, without the compositor.
It goes over about the same.

I still use Otto, exactly as far as I can check, and not a sentence further.
Last week I couldn't remember the name of the little hook under a c.
Otto said cedilla.
I looked, and it was cedilla.
When I need a regular expression, I ask Otto for one.
Before the expression touches anything, I try it on lines it should catch and lines it shouldn't.

I don't ask Otto for dates anymore.
