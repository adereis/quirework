# How's the Book? {#sec-hows-the-book}

Hedda Count always left the book until the end. We would get through the work I was paid to do, agree on what needed doing next, and close our notebooks. Then she would ask.

“How's the book?”

“Chapter four.”

She nodded. I put my pen away.

Hedda was my manager. She was kind and busy, and remembered things people told her. I had told her I was writing a book. This had seemed safe at the time. There was no book to bring in, no page to inspect. I could describe the whole thing without opening a file.

It was called Full Stop, a natural history of punctuation: where each mark came from, and who spread it. I liked saying the title and then pausing before the explanation. I had practiced this enough that the pause sounded accidental.

The premise was small enough to fit into a conversation. There were marks everybody used and mostly took for granted. Someone had put them there. Someone else had copied them. I wanted to follow that business backward, through printed pages and the people who made them, until a familiar speck of ink became a decision again. It seemed like a reasonable book to write in the evenings.

I could still explain the premise after three years. By then I could do it while making coffee, finding a document, or putting on a coat. The description had become much easier to produce than the book.

Full Stop had stopped eleven chapters in. Chapter twelve was the one I owed Red Penn, my editor at a small press. Chapter four was the one I kept opening.

This was not information I included in my answer to Hedda. She had asked a friendly question at the end of a meeting. I gave her a chapter number.

At work I was capable of answering questions about progress. If something was late, I could say what was missing. If I needed help, I could name the person to ask. I could put a date beside a task and, when the date arrived, usually produce something other than a new date. Hedda had evidence of this. So did I.

In our one-on-ones she made room for the things that had gone wrong as well as the things that had gone well. She listened, asked what I needed, and checked the time without making a production of checking the time. The book came after all that. She would leave her notebook shut and give me her attention for another moment.

I began to prepare for that moment. Not formally. I did not write “book answer” on the agenda. I would just find myself, on the way to the meeting, trying to remember what I had done to chapter four since the last time we spoke.

There was always something. I had worked on it. I had sat down after dinner and stayed at the desk until the room was cold. There were changed sentences in the file. If necessary, I could have shown her the changed sentences, though I did not think either of us wanted that.

“Chapter four,” I said.

She smiled, and we went back to work.

I had chosen punctuation partly because the subject was already at hand. I would not need to go anywhere to encounter a comma. The material was in books I owned, books I could borrow, books whose scans I could enlarge until an individual mark filled the screen. An evening's work could begin at my desk and end at my desk. I had considered this an advantage.

My desk acquired the usual arrangement: a book open beside the keyboard, another book underneath that one, a mug in the space where my hand wanted to go. When I needed more room, I moved the mug. When I needed still more room, I put a book on the floor. I was reluctant to close anything. An open book preserved a decision about where to look.

Sometimes I went to bed with the manuscript still open, so I could start quickly in the morning. In the morning I needed the computer for work. I minimized Full Stop. At the end of the day I brought it back. The same paragraph occupied the same part of the screen.

I occasionally selected the entire manuscript to see how many words I had. This was a useful thing to do when I had no new ones. The count included headings, abandoned passages, and instructions to myself to check things. I left those in. They had taken time to write. Then I clicked back into the text, carefully, so I wouldn't replace the whole book with the next letter I typed. This was the closest I came to starting again.

Red was waiting for something I could send, not something I could bring back onto a screen. He had waited three years for chapter twelve. I knew the difference between a chapter and news of a chapter, and so did he.

His name in my inbox made me sit up. I opened his messages promptly. There was no advantage in not knowing what he wanted, since I already knew. He wanted the next chapter of the book he was editing.

I would open a reply, then open the manuscript, on the grounds that I should check before giving him an update. It was possible to spend the time I had meant to use for replying on a sentence instead. By the time I returned to the message, I had worked on the book and still had nothing to attach.

Red had a small press to think about. Hedda had another meeting. Neither had requested an account of how I spent my evenings. I was the one who kept supplying the hours as if they were pages.

I didn't want them to stop asking. When someone asked about Full Stop, I could hear the title outside my own head. For the length of the conversation, it was a book people expected to read. Then I went home and opened chapter four.

I could have sent Red what I had. I rehearsed this possibility away from the desk, where it sounded sensible. I would stop fussing, gather the pages, and let an editor edit. At the desk I wanted to look at one thing first. I did not want to send a problem I had already noticed. That would mean writing a note about the problem. Surely I could spend that time fixing it.

Instead of finishing Full Stop, I eventually built a writing tool called Quirework. That is the detour this book is about. At the time, I would not have called it a detour. I was still sitting at the same desk, with the same book open beside me. I was trying to get chapter four into a state that would let me leave chapter four alone.

But there was no tool yet. There was a manuscript, an editor waiting for the next part of it, and a meeting on my calendar. Before the meeting I saved the file. I had not finished the paragraph, but I had changed it, and I wanted to keep the change.

Hedda and I went through the work. I crossed something off in my notebook. She checked that there was nothing else I needed from her, and I said there wasn't. We closed our notebooks.

“How's the book?”

I had meant to give her a fuller answer. There was time. She was looking at me, not at the clock.

“Chapter four.”
