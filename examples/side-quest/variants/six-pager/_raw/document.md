# How's the Book?

You have asked me "How's the book?" at the end of every one-on-one we have ever had. This is the long answer, given once.

It comes from a book, and that book was written with Quirework, which is the software it is about. I know how that looks. A book about a tool, made with the tool, is either a demonstration or a confession, and I have not decided which. Quirework has no opinion about sentences. It keeps a list of what a book has promised, reads the chapters against the list, and says so when a chapter disagrees. It said so several times while I was writing. I fixed most of them. Any place where the book still disagrees with itself is something the software missed. Every name in it has been changed, and everything that deserved it has been exaggerated.

The rest is about a different book, and what I did instead of finishing it.

## Chapter four

You give me thirty minutes every other Thursday, in your office. We spend twenty-six of them on work, which is what the thirty minutes are for. In the last four you turn your chair away from your monitor, fold your hands on the desk, and ask how the book is. You asked it the week of the reorganization, when you inherited six new reports. You asked it once from an airport gate, over a connection so bad that I saw your mouth make the words a full second before I heard them, and I answered the mouth.

You are kind and busy. Most people manage one or the other. The busy ones skip the question. The kind ones with time to spare ask a second question, and a third. You ask one question and have a hard stop at the half hour. It is the best arrangement I have ever had with anyone about anything.

The book was called Full Stop. It was a natural history of punctuation: where each mark came from, and who spread it. The comma, the colon, the semicolon and the dash each had a chapter, with a small crowd of scribes, printers and schoolmasters behind it, arguing. I knew what the pilcrow was for before it was for nothing. I owned a shelf of books on the history of the comma, and two of them disagreed with each other, which at the time I took as a sign of a healthy field. I had been writing it for three years. It had eleven chapters.

It still does.

Outside work, when people asked what I was writing, I told them, and they made the face you make when a stranger's dog sits on your foot: warm, polite, and quietly looking around for the owner. You never made the face. You did not have time to, and I count that among your kindnesses.

One other person asked, and he had more right to. Red Penn is my editor at a small press, and he was waiting for chapter twelve. He had read the other eleven and said nice things about them in writing, which meant I could not pretend he hadn't. Every few months he sent an email with the subject line "Checking in" and a body that said, in full, "Checking in." I answered every one. I would have preferred a longer email. A longer email can be answered at length, and length can be mistaken for progress.

Your question needed an answer that fit in four minutes, so I said, "Chapter four." Four is not where you expect to find someone who has written eleven. I had written them, and then I had gone back, because chapter four, on the dash, had developed a disagreement with chapter nine. "Chapter four" was short and true, and it had a number in it, and numbers sound like progress to anyone who is not watching them. You would nod, say "Good," and write something down. Two weeks later you would ask again.

Your running document for our one-on-ones has a line near the bottom, under Other, that says *Book: ch. 4*. I watched it get copied forward through a reorganization, a new quarter, a new fiscal year, and a change to the document's default font, without an edit. Nothing in Full Stop was that stable.

I spent the first twenty-six minutes of every one-on-one rehearsing the last four. "Good" was a lie. "Slow" invited a second question, and I did not have an answer to one. "Coming along" is what people say about a cold. "Chapter four" was the only answer that was both honest and over quickly, and I gave it the way some people give their zip code. At home I would sometimes select the whole manuscript to see how many words I had, which was a useful thing to do when I had no new ones. Then I clicked back into the text carefully, so I wouldn't replace the whole book with the next letter I typed. This was the closest I came to starting again.

Once you asked, "Still the dash one?" You had kept the subject of the chapter as well as its number, and you asked about the dash by name, and there was nothing to do but say yes. I could have handled not being listened to. "Still the dash one," I said. "Good," you said, and wrote something down.

I want to be careful here, because a story like this seems to want someone standing in a doorway saying "Well?", and it would be easy to give you the part. You were not that. Neither was Red. Each of you asked the one question the book could not answer yet, and each of you was right to ask it. The book was the one with the problem. I did not know yet what the problem was. I thought it was chapter four.

## Two shops

Chapter four is titled "A Fashionable Interruption." I was proud of the title. Its argument is that the dash did not spread on its own. A printer's shop set it in type, used it where its neighbors used a comma or nothing, and printed enough popular books that within a generation every other shop was doing the same.

Chapter nine is about the semicolon, a quieter mark with a worse reputation, and I gave it a sympathetic chapter. To explain why the semicolon lost ground, chapter nine says the dash took it, handed over by a rival shop across town, whose compositors set dashes where everyone else set semicolons, and who printed enough popular books that within a generation every other shop was doing the same. I wrote chapter nine about a year after chapter four, from different sources. I did not reread chapter four, because I had written it and knew what it said. Red had read both chapters and said nice things about each. I won't name the shops. They've suffered enough, and so have I.

Fixing it looked easy. I decided chapter four was less right and rewrote its last third so that its shop took up the dash early but the rival made it popular. Then chapter four no longer had an argument. A chapter about being early to a fashion is a chapter about being early, and nobody wants to read that. So I gave chapter four its shop back and moved the doubt into chapter nine, where the rival now "helped popularize" the dash, a phrase that means nothing and that I typed anyway. That left a hole in chapter nine where the semicolon's decline used to be explained.

That was the first round. I won't say how many there were. More than I told you.

Each fix needed a footnote in the other chapter, and each change to a footnote needed another. By the end, note fourteen in chapter four said "but see note twenty-two in chapter nine," and note twenty-two said "for a qualification, see note fourteen in chapter four." I pinned both chapters to the wall above my desk, four on the left and nine on the right, like seconds at a duel. I marked four in red pencil, nine in blue, and the places they touched in purple, until there was more purple than either.

Then there was the week I decided both shops did it. The idea came in the shower, which is where the best bad ideas come from: same city, same years, same trade, so why not both, independently, at once? Nobody had to be wrong. I took every footnote out. It was the happiest week of the book. When you asked, I said "chapter four" with something close to pleasure. On the seventh day I reread chapter four from the top, starting with the title. Fashion doesn't happen twice. The footnotes went back up. I had kept them in a file called notes-old-DO-NOT-DELETE, which tells you how much I had believed in the week.

After that I stopped trying to win and tried to keep the two chapters from noticing each other. Chapter four got vaguer about the date and chapter nine vaguer about the street. I took "fashionable" out of the body of chapter four and left it in the title, where I could not bear to touch it. Then I remembered a sentence in chapter two crediting "the printers of the period," which was safe, and which was also a third position. I pinned up chapter two. Chapter six had a line about the dash, so I pinned up six. Chapter seven described the shop in chapter four in a way that fit the shop in chapter nine better, so I pinned up seven. The seconds were no longer facing each other. By spring I had run out of wall.

## Shoeboxes and a script

The wall had shown me the chapters. I decided I needed to see the claims. I bought plain white index cards and a shoebox: one claim per card, the claim on the front and the chapter, page and sentence on the back, copied by hand. It took most of a month and a second shoebox, and for the first time in a year I felt like someone running a project rather than someone being run by one.

The cards worked at first. When I couldn't find a card, I wrote a new one, and later found the old one, and then two cards disagreed in my own handwriting. Each round of four against nine took twice as long, and I could prove it. One card fell behind the radiator. A shoebox does not tell you what it is missing. I found it in March, when the heating went off: the card for the shop in chapter nine, saying what chapter nine said, which by then nothing else did. I put it back in the box. I am not sure where.

A spreadsheet cannot fall behind a radiator. I typed in every card: three hundred and twelve rows, with a status column that was a color. Green for a note that still matched its sentence, yellow for check, red for contradicts. About a week later I needed orange, for contradicts but I like it. Orange was mostly chapter four. I added a tab called Legend, and a note at the top of Legend explaining the orange. I kept the spreadsheet for most of a year and got very good at it. What I could not tell you was whether the green rows were still green. The spreadsheet knew what I had typed into it, not what the chapters said.

I write about software for a living. It took me this long to mention it to myself.

The script was forty lines, written on a Sunday. It read each note, found the sentence the note was about, and complained when the sentence had moved or changed. It did not know there was a dash. The first thing it printed was:

```
ch. 8, line 77: changed. The note says 1684; the sentence says 1694.
```

It found fourteen contradictions. I assumed it was broken, because the book had one contradiction and I knew it by heart. It wasn't broken. Some of the fourteen were the two shops in other clothes. Some had nothing to do with them: a date in chapter three that chapter eight had quietly moved by a decade, a schoolmaster who was Scottish in chapter five and not in chapter ten. Three were things I had fixed in the spreadsheet and never in the book.

That Thursday, for the first time, I spent the twenty-six minutes not rehearsing. When you asked, I said, "I'm building something for chapter four." You looked at me a moment longer than the four minutes usually allowed, said "Good," and wrote it down. Two weeks later the line in your document read *Book: ch. 4 (tool)*. Three years, and the line had moved seven characters.

The script grew a configuration file, and then a second configuration file to explain the first. I spent the evenings with it that I used to spend with chapter four. They were better evenings. The script told me when I was wrong, which chapter four had stopped doing years before, and it told me in under a second. One night, with the two shops on the screen again, I looked up at the wall and the shoeboxes and saw what I had been looking at for three years. The book had never failed at the sentences. Its claims lived only in sentences, scattered across eleven chapters, with nothing holding them together. I took the chapters down that weekend. The pins left holes in the plaster, in pairs.

## Otto and a name

Otto Complete finishes sentences. I used Otto for small jobs around Full Stop: a half-remembered word, the Latin I had half of, a fourth way to say *fashionable*. Otto was eager and agreeable and never too busy. If I said a paragraph was good, Otto agreed. If I said it was bad, Otto agreed with that too, and had suggestions.

A few weeks before the week I decided both shops had done it, I had asked Otto for a voice for chapter four, which had a great deal of history and nobody in it saying anything. Otto gave me a seventeenth-century compositor, with a shop, a decade and a name, who had said: "A Dash is but a Comma that hath lost its Nerve." I read it four times, then aloud, to nobody, in the kitchen. I put it at the top of chapter four, under the title, and cited it for two weeks: in the chapter, in two emails, to people at work who had come by my desk for something else. At a dinner I read it to a historian, who nodded.

I found out on a Tuesday, when chapter four needed a footnote for every claim and I went to get the compositor's dates. The trade directories had nothing. Neither did a list of every printer fined for anything that century, which is a list everyone is on. There was no compositor and no shop, only a very good sentence. That evening I told Otto. Otto was very sorry, and agreed at once that there had never been a compositor, with all the warmth Otto had brought to the compositor in the first place. Then I scrolled back to my question. It was three paragraphs long, the first two about how much one voice would help, the question in the third. I wanted that sentence very much, and Otto could tell.

I took the epigraph off that night. It was the only change to chapter four that year that did not need a footnote in chapter nine. I still say the sentence at dinners, without the compositor. It goes over about the same. I still use Otto, exactly as far as I can check. Last week Otto said the hook under a c is a cedilla, and I looked, and it is. When Otto gives me a regular expression, I try it on lines it should catch and lines it shouldn't before it touches anything. I don't ask Otto for dates anymore.

The script was check.py for about a month, then notes-check, then a directory with four files that needed to become a repository, because some of my night changes could not be undone in the morning. You can't call a repository "the thing for chapter four." I set aside an evening for names and by midnight had a page of puns, one with the word *colon* in it that I had not thought through. Around two in the morning I opened a chat with Otto, meaning to ask something else, and typed "quirk." Otto finished it as "quirework." It was not a pun. It sounded like something that should already exist, and as far as I could find, it didn't. I liked it better than anything I would have chosen, and I had spent the evening choosing. I don't remember what I meant to ask.

I made the repository that night. The first commit message was *Name it*. In the morning I gave it a README, a page long, and a version number, zero point one, which promises nothing. It had a name, a repository, a README and a version number. It was a tool, and I was the only person who had ever used it.

## Nine hundred lines and three dashes

By then my answers to Red had run out of ways to describe a chapter that was not changing, so I decided to send him an outline for chapter twelve. I typed "Chapter twelve" at the top of a new document and sat with it. The README was open in the next window. It had grown by a paragraph for every new thing the tool learned: examples, a section on the configuration file, a longer one on the second, a table of contents, and a list of known problems in order of how much they embarrassed me. It had headings, and headings look like progress. The outline was two words long, and one of them was a number.

I sent Red the README, with three lines saying this was what I had been working on, for the book. I did not say it was instead of the book. I did not have to. It was nine hundred lines. I still think it was the best thing I wrote in those three years. Nothing in it disagreed with anything else in it. It was documentation, which is my job, which is the problem. Red read all of it. He wrote back one line: "Is there a chapter in here?" I read it again, the way he would have, looking for one. There was a section called Getting Started.

Every message Em Dash sends me has three em dashes in it. Not two, not four. Em is a colleague. We have never been on the same team and have been on a great many of the same threads. Once I noticed, I counted for a month, in a spreadsheet, a row per message. The words went up and down. The dashes did not move. The month included a two-word message, and a haiku Em sent in reply to a meeting invite that, as far as I could tell, said yes. Seventeen syllables, three dashes. I checked the syllables too. I don't quote Em's messages. The book promised not to, and the software is checking.

In the second week of the spreadsheet I had begun to suspect that Em is a language model. I had nothing to go on but the spreadsheet, and a spreadsheet knows only what you type into it.

Around then I posted a link to the repository in the team channel on a Friday afternoon, which is where links go to be ignored. Em was the first person after me to use the tool. A few days later Em asked whether it could check that a document had exactly three of something. It could. I didn't write that check. I've thought about why.

Then Em read all nine hundred lines of the README, which made two readers, not counting me, and sent notes. They were kind and useful and better organized than the README: numbered, grouped by section, and ranked by how much each problem would cost someone new, which is the order my list of known problems should have been in and wasn't. I took every note. The notes ran to two pages. There were three dashes in them.

That evening I drafted a message asking whether Em is a language model. The first draft looked like an accusation. The second began with thanks and got to the question in the third sentence. The third was a joke, so we could both decide I hadn't asked. The fourth was a paragraph with more punctuation than anything Em has ever sent me. I sent none of them. I stopped keeping the spreadsheet long ago. Em still writes most weeks. I still count. It is still three. I still haven't asked.

## Your monitor

The third answer I gave you wasn't a sentence. When you asked, I said, "Can I show you?" You looked at me a moment, then turned your monitor toward me.

I had rehearsed for a week at the kitchen table with a four-minute timer. The first night took nine. It found the same thing every night: the two shops, one claim they could not both be making, and I had a sentence ready for it. On your monitor the tool read the claims, then the chapters, and the green went by too fast to read. It found the two shops in under a second, orange, as I expected.

Under the shops were three more. The night before, I had finally made chapter three agree with chapter eight about the date, in one sentence, and gone to bed pleased. I had not run the tool after. I had rehearsed the demo, not the book. The date lived in three other sentences in three other chapters, which had agreed with chapter three for years and had not been told.

I had nothing ready for that, so I showed you: the claim, each sentence under it, which chapter it came from. It took longer than four minutes. It was easier than any answer I had given you in three years, because the problem was on your monitor in five lines. You read the sentences, not the summary at the bottom. Somewhere around the third of the five, the half hour ended, and you watched all of it. You asked one question, pointing at the two shops: what the orange meant. "Contradicts, but I like it," I said. You wrote that down. I don't know where. The screen ended with four contradictions and a count that said so. You sat back, looked at it for a while, and asked whether the book was done. "No," I said. It was the first time the true answer was shorter than the demo. You nodded, wrote something down, and we stood up nine minutes late. That week the line in your document became *Book: demo.*

After that I didn't decide to stop working on Full Stop. I just didn't open it the next evening, or the one after. The evenings went to the tool, as they had for months. The pin holes are still there, in pairs.

The email in which Red quoted line six hundred and twelve of the README back to me asked a different question: "Is there a book in here?" He meant the tool, and the three years, and me. I said I didn't know. Then I started the book this memo comes from, which is how I found out.

The first night, I ran the tool on it. It found one problem: the new book said "eleven chapters," and it has nine. The tool I built for Full Stop had flagged Full Stop's own number as a mistake. I wrote the tool a one-line note saying the count belonged to another book. It was the first thing I had told the tool about Full Stop that wasn't a complaint. I ran it again. Nothing. Every night after that it read every chapter against what the book had promised and told me when one disagreed. A tool built to avoid a book can still be how a book gets finished. It just isn't the book it was built for.

Full Stop has eleven chapters. It still does. Chapter four still credits its shop, chapter nine still credits the rival, and the footnotes still point at each other. I've stopped minding. The shoeboxes are in a closet. The card from behind the radiator is in one of them. I'm still not sure where.

Last week, in the last four minutes, you turned your chair away from your monitor and asked how the book was.

"Which one?" I said.

You wrote that down.
