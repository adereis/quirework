# The young readers' edition

<!-- @skill: Make the young readers' edition of Side Quest, for readers
     of about ten who read well and alone, drafted blind chapter by
     chapter and composed against the spec. -->

Read `variant-common` first: it holds what every edition keeps, and how
an edition is drafted and composed.

The young readers' edition is the same story, chapter for chapter, in
the same three parts, told to a reader of about ten who reads well and
reads alone and has never had a job. Each chapter is drafted blind into
`variants/young-readers/_raw/STEM.md`, and composed as
`variants/young-readers/STEM.qmd` with its own sidecar, keeping the
book's file names and titles.

An edition that is the book with its long sentences split is not an
edition. The book leans on adult things, a manager, a one-on-one, a
README, a fiscal year, version control, and a ten-year-old knows none of
them. Each one is shown in the sentence that uses it, never defined.

Read each chapter against the book for two kinds of failure. The first
is the one a children's book invites: a lesson at the end of a chapter,
a word explained in a parenthesis or a glossary, a feeling named that
the book only shows, a doubt tidied away, a number made rounder or
smaller. The second is the one this brief invites: a replaced joke whose
fact has moved, or a showing sentence that adds an event the book does
not hold. A new joke is built on the book's fact, and the fact stays
exact. Fewer scenes are fine if the story still holds; record in the
sidecar what a chapter leaves out.

## Blind-draft brief

The reader is about ten, reads well, and reads alone, with no adult
beside them to explain anything, and has never had a job. Tell the same
story, with the same people, the same numbers and the same doubts, in
the same first person, to someone who has never been to work.

Where the book leans on an adult thing, show it in the sentence. Hedda
is not "my manager"; she is the person whose job is to help me do mine,
and every other Thursday she has thirty minutes for me, and at the end
she always asks the same thing. Do the same for a README, an editor, a
repository, a configuration file, a spreadsheet's tab, a meeting invite:
say what it is by what it does, in the sentence that uses it. No
definitions, no glossary, no brackets, no aside to the reader. The
sentence does the work.

A joke that only lands for adults becomes a joke a ten-year-old gets,
built on the same fact. A line that survives "a change to the document's
default font" can survive something a ten-year-old would notice. The
fact under the joke stays exact: the same line, the same count, the same
number of years. A joke a ten-year-old already gets stays as the book
tells it, unexplained.

Shorter chapters are fine. Fewer scenes are fine, if the story still
holds. Never talk down: simpler words are welcome, simpler ideas are
not. Add no moral, no lesson and no reassurance. A chapter ends where
the book's chapter ends, on the same moment, without a closing thought
about what it meant.
