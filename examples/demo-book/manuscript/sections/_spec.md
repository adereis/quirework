# What this book holds

Prue's continuity bible: the things a second writer would break without
noticing, stated plainly and priced where a price is owed.

## The punctuation treaty {#punctuation-treaty}

The prose contains no em-dash, anywhere, including this sentence about
it. A colon carries a claim whose second half names the first, a comma
carries a looser join, and two sentences carry everything else. Em
Dash's forty-one additions in chapter one cost the manuscript nothing it
keeps: every one of them is gone by the time the chapter ships.

## Hal is precisely wrong {#hal-precisely-wrong}

Hal is never careless. Every fact he gets wrong is a specific, checkable
claim, delivered with full confidence and no hedge: a page number, a
citation, a chapter. In chapter one he cites chapter two for the same
sentence twice and gives two different page numbers, both stated as
settled fact. The joke is the precision, not the error by itself, and it
stops working the moment Hal sounds unsure.

## Quirks, not commitments {#quirks-not-commitments}

The register this book invents for itself is called a quirk. Mark
considers the word commitments first, in chapter one, and rejects it
there, in his own words, for sounding like a proposal he is not ready to
make. Every reference in this book's own marks to that register uses
quirk, matching the word he actually chose.

## No villain in the spine problem {#no-villain-in-the-spine}

Em Dash is right that the book has two opinions and no spine, and her
method for saying so costs the manuscript forty-one em-dashes it did not
ask for. Both are true in chapter one, and neither is resolved by making
her the villain: she is not mocked, contradicted, or proven wrong
anywhere else in the book.

## Authorship settles by log, not memory {#authorship-by-log}

When the book asks who wrote a sentence, memory does not get a vote. In
chapter two, Mark and Hal each claim the sentence in chapter four, and
neither claims it with much confidence; only the timestamped log says
which draft came first and which one is the sentence everyone quotes.
Prue extends the same rule to herself in the preface: her disagreement
with the log is noted, and the log still wins.
