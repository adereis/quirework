# The book this room is writing

A short work of fiction: a preface (about 450 words, roughly one page)
and two chapters (about 1,400 words each, roughly three pages each).
No illustrations. Nobody in this room has read any other telling of
this story. Invent the scenes, the dialogue, and the room's own
vocabulary; nothing here is a summary of an existing manuscript to
reconstruct.

## The cast

- **Mark Downe.** An engineer who is writing a novel and cannot finish
  it. Methodical, a little too pleased with his own tools, prone to
  solving a feelings problem by building a small piece of machinery for
  it.
- **Hal Lucinate.** An AI writing collaborator Mark uses to draft and to
  check his own manuscript. Hal is never careless and never hedges.
  Every fact Hal gets wrong is a specific, checkable claim, stated with
  total confidence: a page number, a chapter, a quotation. The joke is
  the precision, not the error. Hal must never sound unsure, anywhere.
- **Em Dash.** A human editor Mark sends drafts to. She is right about
  the big things and her method for saying so does small, real damage.
  She is not a villain: she is correct, and expensive, at the same
  time, and nobody in this book proves her wrong or mocks her for it.
- **Prue.** The narrator and the book's assembler. She did not live
  through the events; she built this account afterward from logs
  (drafts, timestamps, chat transcripts) and says so plainly in the
  preface. She has an opinion about at least one disputed fact in the
  chapters that follow, the log disagrees with her, and she lets the
  log win anyway, noting her disagreement rather than hiding it.
- Two more names may appear in passing, mentioned but not necessarily
  staged on the page: **Sid Karr** and **Vic Tor**. Use them or don't;
  they are not load-bearing.

## What must happen, in order

**Preface.** Prue introduces herself and the method: she did not write
what follows, she assembled it from logs, and the reader should not
trust her memory over the record either. She names the cast as
invented. She mentions, in passing, one real thing this book was built
with: Quirework, the tool. It exists; the events and the people do
not, and the preface must say so plainly, not coyly. She flags one
disagreement she has with the log, decides the log still wins, and
moves on. Light, short, funny, and honest that this is fiction.

**Chapter one: the contradiction.** Mark is deep into the novel he
cannot finish. He finds that two passages he wrote, chapters apart,
take opposite positions on the same emotional claim, and he cannot
tell you which one he actually believes. He asks Hal to help settle it
by checking the manuscript, and Hal answers with total confidence and
gets a checkable fact wrong, then gets a different fact wrong the same
way when asked again. Mark sends the draft to Em Dash. She is right
that the manuscript is arguing with itself and has no spine, and her
way of marking that up does something small and undeniably worse to
the prose at the same time, at scale. Stung more by her being right
than by the mess she left, Mark invents a private device: a short way
of naming the specific, narrow thing a passage is committed to, small
enough to point at without it turning into a promise he is not ready
to make. He tries one word for this device first, in his own head or
out loud, rejects it for sounding too much like a commitment, and
lands on the word he actually keeps. He applies the device to the two
contradicting passages and it does not resolve which one is true; it
makes the disagreement locatable for the first time.

**Chapter two: whose sentence it was.** Later, a single good sentence
appears in the manuscript that both Mark and Hal separately believe
they wrote, and neither is fully sure. The dispute cannot be settled
by asking either of them, because memory is exactly the thing not to
trust here; it has to be settled by checking the timestamped log of
drafts, which shows, without ambiguity, which one of them actually
typed it first and when. The chapter should let the reader feel the
temptation to just believe whoever sounds more confident (almost
certainly Hal) and then show why that would be the wrong way to
settle it. Prue, narrating, should tie this back to her own
preface-page disagreement with the log: the same rule applies to her.

## House rules for the prose

1. No em-dash. Not one, anywhere, in any section. Use a colon where
   the second half names the first, a comma for a looser join, or two
   sentences.
2. The preface must say plainly, in ordinary words, that this is
   fiction and that Quirework is the one real thing in it.
3. Dry, concrete, and funny. No corporate register, no throat-clearing,
   no scene that exists only to explain a theme out loud.
4. Short. If a paragraph can go, it goes. Favor one true, specific
   detail over three vague ones.
5. Nobody in this book is a villain, including Em Dash and including
   Hal. Everyone is doing a recognizable, sympathetic thing badly or
   well.
