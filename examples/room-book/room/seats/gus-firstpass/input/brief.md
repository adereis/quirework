# The book this room is writing

    Title:    Chapter and Verse Control
    Subtitle: A short fiction about the engineer who built Quirework
    Author:   Prue Vennance (invented, like everything else here)

A short, funny, entirely fictional story about a software engineer who
could not finish his book and built a tool instead. It ends with the
tool getting its name.

Roughly one page of preface and two chapters of about three pages each.
No illustrations. Nobody here has read another telling of this story:
the beats below are the shape, and the scenes, the dialogue and the
jokes are yours to invent.

## The cast

Every name is a pun on writing or on AI, in the style of "Della Gate"
for a manager or "Addie Min" for a sysadmin. If you need another
character, name them the same way. A name that stops being a pun is a
mistake, like a wrong page number.

    Mark Downe      the engineer. Writes everything in the obvious
                    format. Solves a feelings problem by building a
                    small piece of machinery for it.
    Em Dash         his editor. Returns every draft with forty new
                    em-dashes and one sentence worth the whole pass.
    Hal Lucinate    the model he writes with. Never careless, never
                    hedging, and every fact he gets wrong is a
                    specific checkable claim: a page, a chapter, a
                    date, stated with total confidence. The joke is
                    the precision, not the error. He must never sound
                    unsure.
    Sid Karr        the colleague who rides a motorcycle and supplies
                    the metaphor for where notes belong.
    Vic Tor         draws every diagram as vectors because he does not
                    trust pixels. Mentioned in passing; no diagrams in
                    this edition.
    Prue Vennance   the archivist who wants to know who actually wrote
                    the good sentence. The book is presented as her
                    reconstruction from the logs, which is why she has
                    the byline.

## The shape

**Preface**, about 450 words, in Prue's voice. She did not write what
follows, she assembled it from the logs, and says so. She says plainly
that this is fiction and that Quirework is real. She has a view about
one disputed thing in the chapters that follow, the log disagrees with
her, and she lets the log win while noting that she wanted otherwise.

**Chapter one**, about 1,400 words. Mark's manuscript takes two
opposite positions on the same thing, chapters apart, and he cannot say
which one he believes. He asks Hal, who answers with total confidence
and is precisely, checkably wrong, then wrong again the same way. Em
Dash returns the draft having done something small and real to the
prose at scale, carrying one comment that is worth it and lands harder
than anything Hal said. So Mark starts writing down what his passages
are *for*: one short name per commitment, in a file, with every chapter
saying which ones it expresses. He tries one word for them first and
rejects it for sounding like a promise he is not ready to make, then
keeps the word he actually uses.

**Chapter two**, about 1,400 words. Mark keeps his notes inside the
prose, where he can see them. Hal reflows a paragraph, the notes drift,
and a note ends up confidently describing a paragraph it was never
about, after Mark has already written on the strength of it. Sid Karr,
who edits nothing, supplies the fix as a motorcycle: a sidecar carries
a passenger without changing how the bike leans, and comes off without
stopping the bike. The notes move out of the prose and into files
beside it. Prue turns up wanting to know who wrote the one good
sentence everyone quotes. Mark thinks it was his. Hal thinks it was
his. Neither is confident. The logs are, and they settle it.

The last beat is the name. The sticky note on Mark's monitor has said
"quirk work" since he named the file, something joins it into one word
without asking, and he ships it that way, because renaming a repository
is harder than writing a book.

## House rules for the prose

1. No em-dashes. Not one, anywhere in the prose. It is a plot point.
   Use a colon where the second part names the first, a comma for a
   looser join, or two sentences.
2. The subtitle and the preface say plainly that this is fiction, and
   that Quirework is the one real thing in it.
3. Dry and concrete and funny. No corporate register, no
   throat-clearing, no scene that exists to explain a theme out loud.
   The jokes land on precision, not on whimsy.
4. Short sentences do the work. If a paragraph can go, it goes.
5. Nobody is a villain. Em Dash is right and expensive at the same
   time, and nothing in the book mocks her or proves her wrong.
